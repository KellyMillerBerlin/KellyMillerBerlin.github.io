Building in public

Exploring the problem of Portland parks

November 2026

This was a time-boxed, exploratory scan done in my free time from August to October 2026. My goal was to decide whether problems around families' use of Portland parks were significant enough to justify investing in deeper research and a possible intervention. I drew on my own field notes, desk research, informal conversations with park-goers and friends, and a conversation with one app developer. My conclusion was not to pursue this further for now. The sections below explain how I got there, and where the limits of my sample leave the question open.

Sometime in August I took my kid to a park that was not far from our house for the first time. It had a splash pad, a playground, a dog park, a community garden, and lots of space to roam. It was 9am so although slated to be a hot day, it was still cool enough for this adventure. But when we got there, we discovered that all the play structures, made mostly of metal, were in full sun. Even the swings barely had a shadow in sight. All the trees in the park were also clustered around the dog park, and the splash pad was not operational. Another parent told me that the water turns on sporadically, often only in the afternoon.

I was disappointed and even a bit angry. Should I have prepared better? What would we do now? We found a cluster of very old chestnut trees and spent much of the time playing there in the grass. It ended up being alright. But I ran into this issue often: the park not quite fitting the time of day or the weather, hopes for an easy and simple outing dashed.

Autoethnography

I started taking notes of my frustrating encounters with Portland parks. Mostly these were attributable to my poor planning, which improved the more mistakes I made: misread or outdated pool hours (call first!), poorly chosen bike route (stick to "greenways"!), too-tall structures for my kid's age (look at photos on Google Maps!). I recorded how I felt and what had gotten me here. My kid was often unfazed, happy to play in the dirt wherever we were. But then again, he wasn't the one organizing.

Was this only my problem, I wondered?

Solving a problem that impacts you directly is motivating. But it comes with some risks. Positionality — taking stock of one's place in whatever it is one is studying — tries to get at some of these issues. Since whatever goes unexamined can have undue influence on the approach you take in figuring out something, as well as on the conclusions you draw as a result.

But there is also the myopia of one's own experience. It was totally possible that no one else cared as much as I did about a park with a too-hot slide or monkey bars instead of swings.

Preparatory research

I took a step back and did the often easy but nonetheless important work of desk research. Of course, climate change had made spending time outdoors with children a trickier affair: playgrounds can heat up disproportionately given how they are constructed, making many structures unsafe in the summer. Loss of tree canopy exacerbates the problem, and East Portland has a high incidence of exactly this. But many of Portland's parks are actually fairly shaded, a gift from previous generations where some of the original dense forest was preserved and even hardy trees were planted. If you plan your day right, you can actually find many parks with ample shade.

When it came to overall management, Portland Parks did have a problem and much of it was budgetary. A scathing 2025 audit found a pattern of building new parks and failing to maintain old ones, with 86% of park assets — including playgrounds, restrooms, paths, and community centers — deemed to be in poor or very bad condition. There was no absence of issue-reporting mechanisms. There was simply a friction-filled journey to report them on the one hand and a very long backlog for solving them on the other. To be honest, I had read about the audit when it was published and did not think much of it then, even though I had a young child.

I discovered a few projects that seemed aimed at addressing parks improvement and access. The Portland Parks Atlas tool — a mobile-friendly web application built by Portland Civic Lab — allows users to search by filter and interactive map for maintenance queues. If you want to know if something has been reported for your favorite park and where it sits in the prioritization, this tool is for you. You can also use the tool's features to explore parks by amenities and features, find an event, even reserve a park community space. Whether anyone in Portland uses this tool is a different story, but it does exist. In all the times I looked at the tool, however, I had a hard time imagining someone opening up this interactive map in their browser. Going to a park with your kid is not like planning a vacation or making a big purchase. It is often routine, sporadic, with perhaps a bit of planning from what I have heard and experienced.

I still had to wonder about the impact of the poor quality of Portland parks as reported by the audit. I had run into broken equipment before myself, but before spending more time with the audit report, I didn't take much notice. A rusty swing that squeaked, a seesaw needing a fresh coat of paint. Even a gaping hole in a metal structure where a small foot could easily pass through and get stuck. Somehow in the moment, surrounded by other families, it is easy to ignore the blemishes. I think part of me also assumed that an unsafe structure would immediately be repaired or removed, so public works always had to be safe enough.

Participant observation

After exploring some of the maintenance data in my neighborhood, I visited a park deemed in overall poor condition. It was a sunny day, the breeze moved the pines that give ample shade, the outdoor pool on the edge of the park was loud with laughter. Kids of all ages bounded up and down the structures with abandon. This park was crowded and thriving. Even if noticing now the deterioration of metal and plastic over time gave me pause. It did not seem to be hindering the public's usage. And I did not see anything I felt I needed to report. After all, the backlog was there, we were still using the structures. The city simply hadn't gotten around to repairing them yet.

I spoke to another parent about the quality of the park, if they had noticed its deterioration. "It's not perfect but it's great for us" was the summary of their response. The sophisticated data visualization for park structures in poor condition I had looked up online seemed very far removed from experience on the ground.

I knew I needed to talk to more people, specifically while they were at the park doing whatever it is one does and feeling whatever it is one feels while doing that thing. That is to say, it is easy to consider the impact of things like maintenance in the abstract. But when it is a nice day and you need to get out of the house, what do other parents actually do?

Contextual interviews

I knew better than to ask the kinds of leading questions that sought to confirm the relevance of the problems I wanted to solve. Even if this is very difficult! So I jotted down an informal script and struck up conversations with other park-goers over a period of two weeks. I asked what they liked about the park and what a typical visit looked like. Then I asked if they visited other parks. If they did, what was their reason for visiting that one in particular. I learned that people in general were fairly positive about parks, despite the media coverage critical of the department — in part in response to the notorious audit. Sanitation services keep spaces relatively clean. Sure, they could be more updated. There could be restrooms open year-round. The splash pad could turn on earlier. There could be more shade in the summer. But none of this seemed important enough to elicit more than a shrug when mentioned. We are lucky to have our parks, I heard. Not one person mentioned poor quality of equipment or the notorious maintenance backlog.

Hoping for some richer conversations, particularly with folks in my area, I asked some friends if there were other aspects of their parks experience they would improve. Restroom closures and splash pad hours came up again. This information is accessible online. You can, theoretically, find out which restrooms would be closed in the winter and avoid that park. But would you? I shared the Portland Parks Atlas. No one had heard of it. Perhaps because of the format, but also perhaps because such tools don't fit into our daily routine of decision-making.

Market research

Everyone wants to create an app. Whether web or mobile. It's a container for an idea, it can be designed by anyone in a box, which is often its own problem. So I wondered, did someone have enough frustrating park encounters like me to do just that, build the containerized solution?

The Portland Parks department has its own "explorer", but this is a fairly static and rudimentary website that I cannot imagine anyone actually using.

I did find a mobile app, the Portland Park Passport, that allows users to search by amenities, restrooms, age-appropriate structures and more. It pulls in the same public datasets that form the basis of the Portland Parks Atlas. It has crowdsourced and community features as well. For example, you can check into a park and share location data, you can add photos from your visit, and you can leave ratings. It did not seem to have many users based on the parks I looked at, and there were some functional issues. But the design and the overall intention of the product was professional, cohesive, and seemed to be the start of something great. Mostly I was curious if there was some collaboration with the parks department behind the scenes. Perhaps newly allocated funds as part of a funding push. So I reached out to the developer, a hobbyist who runs an engineering department during the week. He had built the app for his kids entirely on his own, he envisioned physical passports, maybe stamps commissioned by local artists, and ultimately a motivator to enjoy some of Portland's many public parks. I offered some ideas about things I personally would find helpful. But the build was already there, live and waiting to solve something.

I downloaded the app. When I went to a park that week, I added photos and left a review. Then weeks went by, and I never used it again. Each time I went to a park, I noted why I was not using this app and ultimately not seeking out much more information than confirming the location of something on Google Maps. My notes were not surprising: I already knew which parks nearby are the ones I can most easily visit. Going to a park further afield can be logistically challenging. And I have explored so many parks in our quadrant of the city already that I'm not sure I had a use for the app to explore more. At least not yet, with a toddler whose playtime is constrained by nap and meal times.

Not one critical problem but many small ones to solve: system mapping

One fact about Portland that might help explain why the poor quality or variable amenities of some parks may not be such a problem after all is that we live in a city where parks are plentiful and easily accessible: 90% of Portland residents, regardless of income, live within ten minutes of a park. These parks may lack fancy features but many have old-school pits filled with toy trucks and sand toys people simply donate. Many also have paths for bike riding and lots of pine cones for tossing and logs for climbing. My sense is that people are likely to go to whatever park is closest to them, without concern for whether it's not an optimal experience, because they are simply good enough.

This does not mean that there aren't things that can be changed about the pipeline from Portland resident to Portland park experience. And currently there is no application of technology, whether it's the Portland Park Passport or the Portland Parks Atlas, that captures and influences the full picture. Life tends to bleed outside the lines of neat technological containers, and the Portland park experience begins long before the parent looks for a park to visit.

So many factors influence just what this decision-making process looks like: where families live in proximity to parks, the weather of the day (with climate change being a new and significant factor), the accessibility of a park to someone with disabilities or particular needs, and perceptions of safety and community around parks. Whether I return to a park I have decided to visit might also depend entirely on my experience on that given day: did it seem clean, did I feel that I belonged, did it feel safe while I was there, and what of the other children and their parents. That is to say, whether the city has logged the priority of a particular maintenance issue a certain way likely has much less impact than whether there is a sidewalk going from the neighborhood to the park, whether houseless encampments nearby spark fear, or whether dogs are not encouraged, for example. Each family will interact with these many factors differently. And yet Portland still likes its parks.

Once I arrived at the conclusion that I didn't see a clear problem statement to investigate further I realized that I had omitted one crucial aspect from my desk research: qualitative and quantitative data on how Portlanders actually feel about their parks in aggregate. To be sure, I had spent some time on secondary research but it was limited to the outcome of the audit. There is no data publicly available on Portlanders' relationships to their parks since the scathing audit was released. But what I could find from 2010 to 2024 (Portland Vibrant Communities Survey 2024; 2024 Portland Insights Survey; Portland Community Insights Survey 2010) paints a pretty rosy picture of overall satisfaction with parks. It is possible that the audit spurs concern, but from my eyes on the ground, this feels doubtful.

Limitations

I only talked to people already at parks, not families who don't go to parks often or who avoid certain parks, in particular. This would underestimate the friction in getting to a park in the first place. My conversations were also limited to certain days, times, and my part of the city. For longer conversations I chose the convenience of speaking to people I already knew. Many of these people live in the same part of the city as me, are similar in age and other demographic details. My experience as a parent both shaped and limited some of this inquiry. And then there is the question of duration: I only heard but did not experience or observe the issue of winter bathroom closures.

Now that it is fall, we only go to parks when it isn't bitterly cold and raining. So not every day. On the days when the sun breaks through the clouds, the uncovered play areas are a gift. We are surrounded by other children, gobbling up the light. I am grateful for the lack of tree cover at this moment. For those full-sun swings. Rusty or not rusty. Which is also a good lesson in exploring all the seasons and sides of a problem first.

Conclusion

What I learned more than anything in this process was that despite all the issues we face, sometimes that friction is just not enough to warrant a solution at a particular juncture, especially not by a quick technological intervention. Changing routines is hard. And we also put up with a lot if the experience is overall positive and the negative experiences are overall few. In my small way, I tested whether friction was big enough to justify an intervention, and in this sample it just wasn't.

To reiterate, this is not to say that the Portland Parks department doesn't have anything to work on. So many touch points in their provision of services could be improved. I had a recent run-in with poorly communicated information about pool closures. I had checked the website at least twice before packing our bag, telling my toddler we were finally going swimming, and heading to the pool. Standing in a lobby of our local pool with all our gear, disappointed, along with many other families in the same predicament, I could see there was a problem. This gave me an idea for what to look into next.
